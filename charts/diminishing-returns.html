Diminishing Returns

The college wage premium did not collapse. It split in two. The pay gap between degree-holders and everyone else nearly doubled from 1980 to 2000, then went flat for a quarter century while tuition and debt kept climbing. And the labor-market head start a fresh degree used to buy has largely disappeared: recent graduates now face higher unemployment than the workforce as a whole.

What the data says

The premium is still large. Its growth is over. A bachelor’s degree raised typical earnings about 39% above a high-school diploma in 1980. By 2000 that gap had nearly doubled to 79%. In the 23 years since, it has not moved — it sits slightly below its 2000 peak. Tuition and student debt, meanwhile, rose the entire time. A flat premium against a rising price is a falling return, even when the headline number stays high.

The erosion is at the entrance, not the exit. For decades, young graduates were unemployed about 5 points less often than young workers with only a high-school diploma. That gap has fallen to about 2.5 points — the narrowest since the late 1970s. Recent graduates’ unemployment (4.6%) has now risen above the rate for all workers (4.2%), a reversal from 2019. And 42% of recent graduates are underemployed, working jobs that never required the degree.

The degree still works — later. Established graduates, age 28 and up, remain unemployed at just 2.4%, barely changed from before the pandemic. Once college workers are in, they keep more job security and higher pay. What weakened is the fast start the credential used to guarantee: the college grad’s odds of exiting unemployment quickly have fallen roughly ten points since 2000, while the high-school grad’s held steady. The payoff did not vanish. It moved further out, and it stopped rising.
