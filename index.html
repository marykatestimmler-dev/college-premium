The College Premium

What has a bachelor’s degree been worth in the American labor market over the last fifty years, and is that changing? Short version: the degree still pays, and pays most in bad times, but its payoff stopped growing around 2000 and the fast start it used to buy young graduates has largely disappeared.

By Mary Kate Stimmler, built with Claude. Every series is from a U.S. federal statistical agency or Federal Reserve research; the data, sources and caveats are in the repository README. Each chart below has a Chart / Table toggle so the numbers are one click away.

The share of adults with a bachelor’s degree rose about half a point a year for nearly fifty years. Six recessions and nine presidents are not visible in the line.

02Coming of Age

Hold the age constant at 25 to 29 and each point is one generation. From one in seventeen in 1940 to two in five today.

03The Credentialed Majority

Open full page →

Degree-holders as a share of the working labor force, monthly. One in four in 1992; approaching one in two now.

04The Credential Gap

Open full page →

Unemployment for bachelor’s-plus versus high-school-only workers every month since 1992, with the Fed’s policy rate underneath. The gap never closes and widens in every recession.

05Diminishing Returns

Open full page →

The wage premium doubled between 1980 and 2000 and then went flat. The head start a fresh degree used to buy has reversed: recent graduates are now unemployed more often than the workforce as a whole.
